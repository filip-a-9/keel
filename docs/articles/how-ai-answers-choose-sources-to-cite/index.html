How AI answers choose sources to cite, and how to get cited

Published 2026-10-30 by Keel AI Visibility. About 7 minutes.

When ChatGPT answers with web search on, it often cites the pages it used. Those citations are a free dataset: they show which sites the assistant leaned on to describe your market. Nobody outside the model's developer can say exactly how sources are chosen, and anyone who claims a formula is guessing. What you can do is measure what gets cited, then act on the patterns. This article shows how, using real numbers from an independent study.

What is and is not known

The assistant runs searches, reads results and writes an answer that may link to some of the pages. Beyond that, the selection logic is not public and changes with model updates. So treat everything below as observation of what was cited, not a proven account of what causes citation. The useful question is empirical: across many answers to the questions your buyers ask, which domains show up?

The data: 280 answers about project management software

In an independent study of Teamwork.com (October 2026; see the case study), 280 answers from the ChatGPT API with web search, across six themes, were examined for cited pages. Each answer that cited a page or domain more than once was counted once.

Your own pages

Teamwork.com pages were cited in 135 of the 280 answers (48%, interval 42% to 54%). The most-cited were:

  • its agencies page: 29 answers;
  • its pricing page: 27 answers;
  • a support article on standard project budgets: 20 answers;
  • a support article on billing clients: 11 answers;
  • a blog post on agency management software: 9 answers.

Two things stand out. Commercial pages (agencies, pricing) are cited. And so are help-centre articles, which many companies treat as an afterthought. The pages that answer a specific, factual question about the product (how budgets work, how billing works) were cited alongside the marketing pages.

Third-party domains

Leaving out the client and the named competitors, the domains cited most often were:

  • smartsheet.com: 76 answers
  • wrike.com: 54
  • zapier.com: 32
  • float.com: 29
  • getharvest.com: 29
  • capterra.com: 28
  • agencyshed.com: 21
  • productive.io: 20

The mix is informative. Many of the names look like other software products, which probably appear because the assistant mentions or compares them, and others look like publishers and directories. The audit tool cannot tell a brand from a publisher, so a person has to read the list and sort it. It is also worth noticing how concentrated the list is: the top two third-party domains alone appeared in 76 and 54 of 280 answers, so a handful of sites account for a large share of the outside sources.

How to turn citations into a plan

1. Sort the domains

Split the cited third-party domains into competitors or adjacent products, review and comparison sites, and independent publishers. Competitors you cannot influence. Review sites and publishers are where coverage is at least possible.

2. Check whether you are present on the ones that matter

For each review site or publisher that appears often, ask: are we listed? Is the listing accurate and current? Is there an article that compares tools in our category, and are we in it? Listings on directories such as Capterra are often free or low cost to claim, though this varies by site and you should check each one's terms.

3. Look at the gap themes, not the average

Citations are most useful on the themes where you are weak. In the Teamwork.com study the weak theme was resource planning, where the brand appeared in 25% of answers against 85% for Asana. Among Teamwork.com's own pages, a blog post on resource-planning tools was cited in 5 answers and a support article on the workload planner in 1. The domain counts above are across all 280 answers, not per theme, so they show where outside sources come from in general. The next cut, which is worth asking for in any audit, is the domains cited on the weak theme alone, because that is where an answer is being assembled from sources that may not feature the brand. Filtering citations by theme, not overall, is the step most people skip.

4. Make your own pages answerable

If the assistant cites help-centre articles, make sure the ones covering your weak themes exist, are specific, and say plainly what the product does. A page that states what a feature does, who it is for and how it works is easier to use as a source than a page that only sells. This is a reasonable hypothesis consistent with what was cited, not a guarantee.

5. Earn coverage the normal way

For publishers, the route is ordinary: be genuinely useful to their readers, provide accurate information to the writers who compare tools, and contribute data or expertise they can cite. Paying for placement is possible on some sites, but it needs to be disclosed according to the site's rules and UK advertising rules. Do not manufacture reviews. Fake or incentivised reviews that are not disclosed can breach UK consumer law and will damage trust if discovered.

What you should not expect

  • No guarantee. Getting a listing or an article does not guarantee a mention, because the assistant may still not use it.
  • Stability. Citation patterns change as models and indexes update. Re-measure rather than assume.
  • Causation. The fact that a site is cited often does not prove it drives the mention. It is a lead.

A short checklist

  • List the cited third-party domains and mark each as competitor, directory, publisher or other.
  • For each directory and publisher, note whether you are present and whether the entry is accurate.
  • Filter by your weakest theme before deciding where to spend effort.
  • Check that your own help and blog pages answer the specific questions on that theme.
  • Record the baseline rate and interval, then re-measure with identical questions.

How to measure whether it worked

Before changing anything, record the baseline mention rate and 95% interval for the weak theme, using the same questions and settings. After the work, and after enough time for pages to be published and indexed, run the same questions again. If the new interval does not overlap the old one, you have evidence of a real change. If it does, you cannot tell yet. Why intervals matter explains the reasoning.
